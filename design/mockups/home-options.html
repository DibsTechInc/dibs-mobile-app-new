Home — three compositions

Same components, same tokens, three different points of view about what a client wants when they open the app. Each shown in both launch studios. Pick one to build, or tell me which parts of which to combine.

→ Booking walkthrough & account

Option A — The Cover

The studio's photograph is the first thing you see, nearly half the screen, with the studio name set over it in Fraunces. Underneath, your next class, then today's schedule. It reads like the cover of a magazine that happens to let you book.

What it buys: maximum brand presence — a studio owner opening this feels like they hired a design studio. What it costs: the photo pushes real content below the fold, so a client checking tomorrow's schedule scrolls past a picture they have seen 200 times. Watch: Everyday Ballet's hero is high-key, so white text on it needs the scrim to work harder than Carlsbad's does.

Option B — The Agenda

Leads with the thing a returning client actually came for: what is next, and when. The time is a Fraunces hero numeral — the single biggest element on the screen. The photograph becomes a slim band behind the header rather than the header itself.

What it buys: the fastest possible answer to "when is my class", and it reads as confident rather than decorative. What it costs: less brand theatre on open — the studio's photograph is a supporting element, not the star. Note: this is the composition that degrades best when a client has nothing booked (the block becomes an invitation to book, same size, same position).

Option C — The Split

Asymmetric. The photograph occupies a tall offset block on the right; the greeting and next class sit against it on the left, overlapping the edge. Content determines the layout rather than a grid — the composition changes shape depending on whether you have a class booked.

What it buys: the most distinctive of the three, and the least likely to be mistaken for any other booking app. What it costs: the most fragile — it depends on the studio photo having a workable subject position, and a portrait shot with a centred subject fights the crop. It is also the hardest to keep coherent as content grows. My read: gorgeous for a launch screenshot, riskier as the screen someone opens daily.

My recommendation

Option B, with Option A's hero kept for the very first launch after install. The photograph earns its space once — as the studio's cover on day one — and after that a returning client is opening the app to answer one question, which B answers in the first 200 pixels. B is also the only one of the three whose empty state is as good as its full state, which matters more than it sounds: most clients have nothing booked most of the time.

Option C is the one I would want in the App Store screenshots. If you love it, the honest compromise is C's composition on Home and B's discipline everywhere else — but it will need a photo brief for future studios, because it only works with a subject that sits off-centre.
